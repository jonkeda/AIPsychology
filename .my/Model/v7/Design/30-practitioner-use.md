# Practitioner Use

How the model can actually be used, by whom, and where it stops being useful. Written for managers, coaches, L&D and change practitioners, therapists working with professional identity, and researchers.

The model is unvalidated. Every use below should be read as *this is a way to have a better conversation*, not *this is a way to know something about a person*. The distinction is the whole of the guidance.

## The one rule

**The model is for understanding where someone is. It is not for deciding where they should go.**

Position labels describe. They do not license pushing anyone through a transition, and they do not identify a destination — there is no target position, and a person who stays in `SB` Scope Boundary for a decade has not fallen short of anything.

The failure mode this rule prevents is specific and common: a manager learns the vocabulary, decides a team member is "stuck in `GE` Guarded Evaluation", and uses the label to justify pressure they had already decided to apply. The model has then supplied an authoritative-sounding wrapper for something it explicitly does not support. Any use where the position label makes a predetermined action sound more legitimate is a misuse.

# The core skill: think in domains, not people

The most common mistake is asking *which position is this person in?* The question has no answer.

The right first question is **which domain are we talking about?** Then the position question becomes answerable and the answer becomes useful.

A senior designer might be:

| Domain | Stakes | Ceiling | Position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Concept and art direction | High | D1 | `GE` Guarded Evaluation: entrenched |
| Production and asset variants | Moderate | D4 | `IP` Integrated Practice: mastery |
| Client presentations | Low | D5 | `CU` Calibrated Use |
| Written proposals | Low | D5 | `OU` Overuse: efficiency |

Four positions, one person, one day. "She's resistant to AI" is false about three of these and misleading about the fourth. The domain-level picture immediately suggests where a conversation is worth having and where it is not.

## Finding the domains

Three steps, in order. Domains are identified **before** any AI discussion, so that the carve-up is not contaminated by where the person's AI use happens to sit.

**Step 1 — Work history scan.** *"Walk me through what you actually produce in a typical week."* Outputs, not job titles. Follow with: what took longest to get good at, what required formal training, and what they would say they are known for.

**Step 2 — Consolidation.** Group by skill family. Aim for two to six domains. Fewer loses the multi-domain picture; more becomes unusable in conversation. Split where identity investment genuinely differs, not where tasks merely differ.

**Step 3 — Verification.** Present the list back. *"Does this feel like how your work divides up?"* The corrections are informative — where a person insists on splitting, investment usually differs; where they merge, it usually does not.

## Assessing stakes before positions

Identity Stakes must be assessed **before** any position discussion and from evidence independent of AI use. This is not academic rigour transplanted into practice; it is what stops the practitioner confirming their first impression.

Ask, before AI comes up at all:

- How long in this domain?
- What share of the work, and of the income?
- What training went into it?
- Would others describe them as a person who does this?
- Did they name it unprompted when describing their work?

Record the assessment. Then move to positions. A stakes judgement formed after hearing that someone dislikes AI is worthless, because it will be the judgement that explains the dislike.

# Protocols

## Protocol 1 — The orientation conversation

**For:** a manager or coach with one person, 45–60 minutes. The default use of the model.

**Before:** domains identified, stakes assessed, both recorded. Model vocabulary not shared.

**During, per domain in order of stakes:**

1. *"When you use AI for this, what does it do well?"* — establishes whether an assessment exists.
2. *"When has it disappointed you?"* — specificity here is the signal. Vague answers suggest low engagement; highly specific answers suggest real testing.
3. *"Tell me about a time it impressed you and changed your mind about something."* — the `OE` Open Evaluation versus `GE` Guarded Evaluation probe. A specific answer indicates symmetric updating; an answer immediately discounted indicates the opposite.
4. *"Where do you draw the line — what do you keep for yourself?"* — surfaces the boundary.
5. *"If a model came out next month that was noticeably better at that part, what would you do?"* — the `SB` Scope Boundary versus `IP` Integrated Practice: mastery probe. Immediate movement or immediate refusal both point to `SB` Scope Boundary; "I would have to test it" points to `mastery`.
6. *"How much of your week is now checking output rather than making it?"* — Verification Burden. Ask about volume received versus volume reviewable.

**After:** record position, attributes, and confidence per domain. Confidence is not optional — a position recorded without it will be read as fact within a fortnight.

**Do not** share position labels with the person during the conversation. Once someone knows the vocabulary, subsequent conversations become a performance of it, and the practitioner loses the only instrument they had.

## Protocol 2 — Team mapping

**For:** a manager or change practitioner with a team of 5–30.

**Method.** Identify two to four domains shared across the team. Assess each person per shared domain using Protocol 1 or a shortened version. Record in a grid: people as rows, domains as columns, position in cells.

**What to read from the grid:**

| Pattern | Reading | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One domain, most people in `GE` Guarded Evaluation | The threat is in the domain, not the people | Address the domain-level question, not individual attitudes |
| Wide spread within one domain | Normal and healthy | Nothing. A team where everyone is in the same position is the anomaly |
| Cluster in `OU` Overuse: mandate or `OU` Overuse: social | The organisation is manufacturing the behaviour | Change the incentive, not the people |
| Cluster in `OL` Oversight Load: exceeded | A workflow problem, not a people problem | Redesign review; nothing individual will help |
| Several `CU` Calibrated Use: coerced | The rollout looked successful and was not | Expect `GE` Guarded Evaluation, `OU` Overuse: mandate, or `BO` Burnout in the following months |
| One person in `IP` Integrated Practice + `AV` Advocacy and the rest in `GE` Guarded Evaluation | The advocate is probably making it worse | Reduce the advocacy's visibility before addressing anyone's position |

**The most valuable single output** is usually the mandate cluster. When most of a team is in the same driven position, the cause is upstream of every individual, and individual conversations are wasted effort.

**Never** put the grid in a performance system, share it with anyone outside the assessment, or let position labels reach a promotion discussion. If the grid could plausibly leak into an evaluation process, it should not be created.

## Protocol 3 — Exit triage

**For:** someone who has stopped using a tool, or stopped engaging with a workflow.

**Method.** Score both severity axes independently.

- **Practical severity (0–10):** how badly did the tool fail their actual needs?
- **Emotional severity (0–10):** how much identity pressure or exhaustion drove the exit?

Then run the **reversibility check**: offer a tool that solves the specific failure they cited. If they take it up, practical severity was driving. If they do not, emotional severity was, and the practical reason was the acceptable thing to say.

**Response by quadrant:**

| | Low emotional | High emotional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low practical** | Often leave it. Opportunity cost or boredom, and not every non-adoption is a problem | Support and slow re-exposure. Better tools will not touch this |
| **High practical** | Tooling, defaults, training. Straightforward | Fix the tool first, then the rest. Offering support while the tool is genuinely broken reads as being told the problem is in their head |

The bottom-right ordering matters more than anything else in this protocol. Support offered before a real practical problem is fixed damages trust that is then unavailable for the harder conversation.

## Protocol 4 — Rollout pacing

**For:** anyone introducing AI tooling to a group.

The model's clearest practical contribution, because it explains a failure that is otherwise mysterious: rollouts that look successful and unravel two quarters later.

**The mechanism.** Forcing delegation above a person's ceiling produces compliance that looks like calibration. Nothing underneath has changed. It resurfaces as `GE` Guarded Evaluation, `OU` Overuse: mandate, or `BO` Burnout, long after everyone concluded the rollout worked.

**What follows:**

1. **Assess ceilings before mandating levels.** A team with D2 ceilings in a core domain will not reach D4 because a deadline says so.
2. **Mandate the floor, not the ceiling.** Requiring exposure is reasonable. Requiring a delegation level is what produces coerced `CU` Calibrated Use.
3. **Allow stance formation.** People who are given room to evaluate reach a stable position faster than people who are not. Compressing the Stance family does not skip it; it defers it.
4. **Watch verification burden as capability rises.** The predictable failure of an agent rollout is not adoption refusal. It is the review load arriving three months later at a volume nobody planned for.
5. **Treat high-stakes domains separately.** The same rollout produces `CU` Calibrated Use in a low-stakes domain and `IS` Identity Shock in a high-stakes one. Uniform rollouts to non-uniform stakes are the single most common design error.

# What to do, position by position

Descriptive support, not prescription. The right response depends on the person, the domain, and their circumstances — this is a list of what tends to go wrong.

| Position | Common mistake | Better |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `NE` No Engagement: closed | Treating non-engagement as resistance | There is no stance to argue with. Manufacturing one creates the problem |
| `FC` First Contact | Mandating the first encounter | Let it be voluntary if at all possible. Mandated `FC` First Contact routes to `GE` Guarded Evaluation |
| `OE` Open Evaluation | Interrupting the testing with advocacy | Leave them alone. This is the position doing exactly what it should |
| `GE` Guarded Evaluation | Arguing with evidence | Evidence is what the position is built to discount. Address stakes, or wait for an event |
| `VR` Values Refusal | Treating it as a competence problem | It is not one. Test consistency across domains before assuming anything |
| `IS` Identity Shock | Reassurance, or pressure to move on | Neither. Acknowledge the loss is real. If severe or beyond several months, refer to a clinician |
| `SB` Scope Boundary | Trying to move the boundary | The boundary is load-bearing. It moves when the person is ready, on evidence |
| `CU` Calibrated Use: coerced | Reading it as success | Expect it to unravel. Check the ceiling that was overridden |
| `OU` Overuse: anxiety | Praising the output | Praise the person's own contribution, specifically. The driver is self-trust that transferred |
| `OU` Overuse: mandate | Individual coaching | The cause is organisational. Coaching an individual out of a mandate is not possible |
| `OL` Oversight Load: exceeded | Time management advice | The volume is the problem. Redesign review, or reduce what arrives |
| `IP` Integrated Practice + `AV` Advocacy | Amplifying the advocate | Check the grounding first. Enthusiasm-based advocacy makes `GE` Guarded Evaluation worse across a team |
| `BO` Burnout | Treating it as capability failure | Recovery is structural. Reduce load; do not re-skill |
| `SD` Structural Displacement | Treating it as a psychological problem | It is an economic one. Career support, not identity work |
| `DX` Disengagement | Assuming the stated reason is the real one | Run the reversibility check |

# Uses by role

## Managers

- **Do:** work out which domain a disagreement is actually about before having it; distinguish organisational drivers from individual ones; pace rollouts against ceilings; notice `OL` Oversight Load before it becomes `BO` Burnout.
- **Do not:** put positions in performance reviews; use labels to justify a decision already made; assess someone's whole relationship to AI from one conversation about one domain.

## Coaches and L&D

- **Do:** use the vocabulary to give a client language for something they could not previously name; separate the identity question from the tooling question; use the boundary probe to help someone examine whether their line is where they think it is.
- **Do not:** share position labels with the client early — it converts every subsequent session into a performance; set a target position; work with severe `IS` Identity Shock without clinical support.

## Therapists

- **Do:** use the model as a frame for professional identity disruption where the client's presenting material is occupational; recognise `IS` Identity Shock as a describable experience with a name, which is itself often useful to a client.
- **Do not:** treat it as a clinical instrument. It has no validation, no norms, and no evidence base. It is a vocabulary for a conversation that a trained clinician is already competent to have.

## Researchers

- Everything in [20-testing.md](20-testing.md). The single most useful contribution available from outside is an inter-rater reliability study, because it is cheap and because a negative result would improve the model more than any positive result could.

## Individuals, on themselves

The model is usable for self-orientation with two cautions.

- **The domain framing is the useful part.** "I am anxious about AI" is less tractable than "I am guarded about the one domain where I have the most invested, and relaxed about four others." The second is a workable observation.
- **Self-assessment cannot see `OU` Overuse: anxiety or `OL` Oversight Load: rubber-stamping.** These are exactly the positions people cannot report about themselves, because reporting them is ego-threatening. A person who concludes they are not in either should hold that conclusion loosely.

# Limits

## What the model cannot do

- **Diagnose.** No validated instrument places anyone anywhere. Every classification is a practitioner's judgement wearing a code.
- **Predict.** The graph says an edge exists. It does not say who takes it or when.
- **Measure.** The 0–10 severity axes have no established inter-rater reliability. They organise a conversation; they are not data.
- **Generalise across cultures.** The cultural material is conjecture with no supporting data.
- **Evaluate.** Nothing in the model says which position anyone should be in. The sustainability annex is the author's opinion and is labelled as such.

## Where it will mislead

| Situation | Failure |
| --- | --- |
| Assessing a person rather than a person-in-a-domain | Produces a single label that is wrong about most of their work |
| Assessing in a mandated environment | Usage and enthusiasm are both compromised as signals |
| Assessing someone who knows the model | The answers are a performance of the framework |
| One conversation | `OE` Open Evaluation versus `GE` Guarded Evaluation and `SB` Scope Boundary versus `IP` Integrated Practice: mastery need repeated observation. A single sitting cannot separate them |
| Assistive users, or involuntary AI subjects | Out of scope. The vocabulary will produce confident misdiagnosis |
| AI-native practitioners | The Disruption family may not apply at all |

## Three practices that keep the model honest

1. **Record confidence with every classification.** A position noted without a confidence marker becomes a fact in the reader's memory within a fortnight.
2. **Re-assess after any `⚡` event.** Capability releases, mandates, tool deprecations, and access changes all invalidate prior classifications, particularly in high-stakes domains.
3. **Keep positions out of any system with consequences.** The moment a position label can affect pay, promotion, or headcount, everyone learns which positions are rewarded and the model stops measuring anything at all.
