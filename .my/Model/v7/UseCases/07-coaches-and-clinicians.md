# Use Cases — Coaches and Clinicians

Scenarios for executive coaches, career coaches, organisational consultants, and therapists whose clients bring occupational material.

This group is worth assessing separately for a specific reason: they are the only readers whose professional practice is built around holding an interpretive framework lightly. Coaches and therapists are trained to use models as lenses rather than as measurements, which makes them the audience least likely to misuse this one — and correspondingly the audience where its value is most likely to be realised.

---

## S1. The client who cannot name what happened

**Situation.** A senior copywriter tells her coach that something changed a few months ago and she has not felt the same about the work since. She cannot be more specific. She mentions, in passing, watching a colleague generate in a few minutes something comparable to what she would have spent a day on.

**Without the model.** The coach works with it as a general motivation or meaning-in-work issue. That is not wrong, and it may help. But the specific mechanism stays unnamed, and the client continues to interpret the experience as a loss of drive.

**What the model supplies.** A name and a shape. The felt value of accumulated skill dropped sharply against a specific event. This is a recognised response, it is not a motivational failure, and it has a typical course.

**What changes.** The client stops interpreting it as something wrong with her, and the work becomes about recalibrating what her skill now consists of rather than about recovering lost enthusiasm.

**Is the difference real?** Yes. This is the model's strongest case anywhere in the audit. Naming an experience that a client cannot name, and locating its cause outside their character, is a genuine mechanism and it is the thing coaching does. The model supplies precise vocabulary for a phenomenon that is currently discussed in generalities.

---

## S2. Distinguishing two clients who present identically

**Situation.** Two clients say something close to *I can't do what I used to be good at any more.*

**Without the model.** Both are worked with as confidence or identity issues.

**What the model supplies.** They may be entirely different situations. One is an identity problem — the work still exists, the person's sense of themselves has not caught up, and the useful work is recalibration. The other is a structural problem — the market for that work is genuinely closing, and the useful work is economic planning.

**What changes.** Potentially the whole direction of the engagement. Doing identity work with someone whose profession is closing is a well-intentioned way of helping them not act while their position erodes.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and the failure it prevents is serious. But the model must be clear about its limit: it supplies the distinction and cannot determine which case applies. That is a judgement about a labour market, and the model explicitly declines to make it.

## S3. The client who wants permission

**Situation.** An illustrator has decided not to use generative tools in his own work. He is confident about the decision and keeps returning to it, seeking something from his coach he cannot articulate.

**Without the model.** The coach explores the decision, possibly probing whether it is avoidance. The probing is reasonable and may be experienced as pressure, because the surrounding culture already frames any limit as resistance.

**What the model supplies.** A drawn line is a stable, common, and frequently sensible position rather than a failure to adapt. The useful question is not whether to hold the line but what it is drawn around — competence or identity — and either answer is legitimate.

**What changes.** The coach stops implicitly treating the decision as a problem, and the client can examine it without defending it.

**Is the difference real?** Moderate. A good coach would not pathologise the decision anyway. The model's contribution is countering an external framing that the client has already absorbed, which is worth something but is not a new capability.

---

## S4. The high performer who is quietly depleted

**Situation.** A client is delivering well, is praised, and describes the work as hollow. He mentions approving a great deal of material he has not really read.

**Without the model.** Explored as meaning, values, or burnout precursors. Reasonable, and it may miss the specific mechanism.

**What the model supplies.** The particular corrosion of approving work one has not checked, while knowing it. This is not ordinary overwork and it is not imposter feeling — it is a sustained low-grade integrity compromise, and it has a structural cause that is not the client's fault and may not be within the client's control.

**What changes.** The exploration becomes specific. Distinguishing what the client can change from what is a property of the role also determines whether the useful conversation is about coping or about leaving.

**Is the difference real?** Yes. This is a genuinely under-described experience, and the vocabulary is not otherwise available.

---

## S5. Knowing when to stop

**Situation.** A career coach's client describes something more severe — months of low mood, withdrawal, loss of professional motivation with no sign of lifting.

**What the model supplies.** An explicit boundary. Sustained severe disruption is outside what the framework describes and outside what coaching should hold. The model says so and says to refer.

**Is the difference real?** Modest but genuine. A framework that marks its own limit is better than one that does not, and coaching frameworks frequently do not. The value is in the boundary being stated at all.

---

## S6. The therapist with a client whose profession is changing

**Situation.** A therapist has a client whose presenting material is occupational — professional identity, competence, and worth, in a domain being reshaped by AI.

**Without the model.** The therapist works with identity and self-worth using their existing training, which is entirely adequate for the psychological content and may lack purchase on the specific occupational situation.

**What the model supplies.** Domain-specific context. What is actually happening in that profession, why the response is common rather than idiosyncratic, and that the client's assessment of their situation may be accurate rather than distorted — which matters, because treating an accurate appraisal as a cognitive distortion is a real risk here.

**What changes.** Contextual understanding, not technique. A trained therapist does not need the model to do the work.

**Is the difference real?** Modest. The model is background reading, not an instrument, and it should be explicit about that.

# Assessment for this group

## Where the value is

| Contribution | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| Vocabulary for experiences clients cannot name | Strongest. This is the model's best use anywhere |
| Normalising a response that clients read as personal failure | Strong, and a real mechanism |
| Identity problem versus structural problem | Strong, with a firm limit |
| The specific corrosion of unverified approval | Strong, and not otherwise described |
| Domain context for practitioners outside the sector | Moderate |
| Stated referral boundary | Modest but worth having |

## Why this group is the best fit

Three properties align well:

- **Coaching and therapy are conversations, and the model's output is conversational.** There is no requirement to classify anyone, and the vocabulary does its work in the exchange itself.
- **The practitioners are trained to hold frameworks lightly.** This is the audience least likely to convert a description into a measurement.
- **No employment consequence attaches.** A coach's working hypothesis does not affect anyone's pay or role, which removes nearly every harm identified in [08-harms-and-net-value.md](08-harms-and-net-value.md).

**These are also the conditions under which the model's lack of validation matters least.** A lens that helps a conversation is judged by whether the conversation goes better, not by whether it corresponds to a measurable reality. This is the one audience for whom the honest framing — a way of paying attention, not a measurement — is exactly what they wanted anyway.

## Where the risks are

- **Amateur clinical application.** Career coaches without clinical training encountering genuine distress. The referral boundary must be prominent, not parenthetical.
- **The framework becoming a lens the client is fitted into.** The expressiveness trap, in a setting where the practitioner has interpretive authority and the client is inclined to accept the interpretation.
- **Selling it as an assessment.** A coach who offers AI adaptation assessment as a service has converted a lens into a product, and the product does not exist.

## The honest summary

**Coaches and clinicians are the model's best audience, and the audience its current form serves worst.**

They need vocabulary, boundaries, and context. They do not need a graph, positions, attributes, an observation guide, or a classifier — and several of those actively invite the misuse that this group is otherwise least likely to commit.

The material this audience would use is short: perhaps six named experiences, the domain framing, the identity-versus-structure distinction, and a clear referral boundary. That could be a single readable document.

**A secondary observation worth noting.** This group is also the most likely to give useful feedback, because they will apply the vocabulary immediately and will notice quickly when it does not fit a client. For the cheap-evidence programme in [Plans/03-cheap-evidence.md](../Plans/03-cheap-evidence.md), coaches are the highest-value early readers — and the fastest route to finding out whether anyone returns to the material a second time.
