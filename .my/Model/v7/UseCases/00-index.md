# Use Cases — Index

These documents address one question: **does this model have enough value to justify continuing?**

They are written as an audit, not as advocacy. The question was asked seriously and the answer is allowed to be no.

## The verdict, stated first

**The model has real value, but substantially less than its current size implies, and it is concentrated in about four of its components.**

Three findings drive this:

1. **A small part of the model does nearly all the useful work.** Domain-specificity, identity stakes, delegation levels, and verification burden account for almost every case where the model changes a decision. The fourteen-position graph — the part that took the most effort — accounts for very few, and carries most of the risk of harm.

2. **Most of the model's components already exist elsewhere, with better evidence.** This is initially bad news and ultimately good news. Automation research has covered oversight load, trust calibration, and levels of delegation for four decades. Grounding the model in that work would import an evidence base at zero cost and convert it from *unvalidated framework* to *established constructs applied to a new context*.

3. **The realistic value is packaging, not discovery.** The people who need this material will never read the automation literature. A framework that brings it to them in usable form is worth something. That is a more modest claim than the model currently makes, and it is defensible.

**Recommendation: do not stop. Stop expanding.** Shrink the model to what earns its place, ground it in prior work, and ship something short and practical before building anything else. Detail and a stopping rule are in [09-verdict.md](09-verdict.md).

## A note on the standard being applied

The question "does this have value" is easy to answer badly in either direction. Two failure modes are avoided deliberately throughout:

**The advocacy failure.** Listing everything the model could conceivably be used for, without asking whether anyone would actually use it or whether they already have a way of doing the same thing.

**The nihilism failure.** Concluding that because nothing is validated, nothing is worth anything. Most useful professional frameworks are unvalidated. The relevant question is not whether the model is proven but whether it beats the alternative available to the person in the moment — which is usually no framework at all.

The test used throughout is the **counterfactual**: what would this person have done without the model, and is the difference worth the cost of the model existing?
