# Use Cases — Individuals

Concrete scenarios for the person actually using AI in their work. Each states what happens without the model, what the model adds, and whether the difference is real.

All scenarios are constructed. Constructed scenarios favour the framework being assessed, and the counterfactuals are estimates. Both should be discounted.

---

## S1. The unease with no name

**Situation.** A developer with eighteen years of experience spends a week supervising an agent that implements a substantial feature. The work is good. It passes review. She is unsettled in a way she cannot account for, and concludes she is being precious about it.

**Without the model.** The unease is interpreted as a character flaw — resistance to change, ego, failure to keep up. This interpretation is common, it is wrong, and it is corrosive because it converts a situational response into a verdict about the person. It also produces the worst available action, which is to suppress the signal and continue.

**What the model supplies.** The situation is a high-stakes domain at D5, where the person cannot locate their own contribution. The unease is a recognised response to a specific structural feature of the work — responsibility retained, observation lost — rather than evidence about her character.

**What changes.** Two things. The self-attribution shifts from *something is wrong with me* to *something specific is different about this arrangement*. And a lever becomes visible: drop to D3 or D4 for the parts that carry the identity weight, retain D5 for the rest.

**Is the difference real?** Yes. Naming a response and locating its cause outside the self is a genuine mechanism, not a placebo. Someone with a good coach or a perceptive colleague might get here without the framework, but many people have neither, and the default interpretation is the damaging one.

**Which component did the work.** Delegation levels and identity stakes. The position graph contributed nothing.

## S2. Exhausted, with reasonable usage

**Situation.** A technical writer is depleted at the end of each day. His own AI use is modest and well judged. He assumes he is not coping.

**Without the model.** Diagnosed as a personal capacity problem. Remedies attempted: time management, focus techniques, working longer. None address the cause, and their failure confirms the self-diagnosis.

**What the model supplies.** Usage is not the variable. Review volume is. Most of his day is checking material he did not write, arriving faster than he can read it at the standard he holds. The relevant question is not how he uses AI but how much output he is accountable for.

**What changes.** The problem relocates from the person to the workflow, which makes it addressable: sampling instead of exhaustive review, automated checks, a stated review budget, or a conversation with a manager framed as capacity rather than as struggling.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and this is among the model's strongest cases. The misattribution it corrects is specific, common, and expensive, and the correction points at an intervention that works.

**Which component did the work.** Verification burden alone.

## S3. The person who thinks they are bad at AI

**Situation.** A designer uses AI comfortably for scheduling, research, copy, and client correspondence, and refuses it for concept work. Her team's adoption dashboard marks her as a low adopter. She has begun to describe herself as behind.

**Without the model.** A single-number view of a person who is in fact fluent in four areas and deliberate in one. The self-description hardens, and the organisation's measurement has manufactured a problem that did not exist.

**What the model supplies.** Five domains, four at D3 or above, one held at D1. That is not low adoption. It is a person who has drawn a line around the thing she trained for, which is a stable and defensible position rather than a deficit.

**What changes.** She stops describing herself as behind. The line becomes something she chose rather than something she is failing at, and she can examine it deliberately rather than defensively.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and it is the model's clearest single case. The correction requires no theory beyond domain-specificity, and the harm it undoes was created by measurement that ignored domains.

**Which component did the work.** Domain-specificity alone.

## S4. Deciding whether to hand over the thing that matters

**Situation.** An architect can now delegate schematic design to a system that produces credible output. She does not know whether declining is professional judgement or fear.

**Without the model.** The question is decided by whichever framing is more available — usually the organisational one, which treats any limit as resistance. She either delegates against her judgement or refuses without being able to say why.

**What the model supplies.** A usable test. Is the line drawn around what she is known for, or around what the tool is actually unreliable at? And: if a better system appeared next month, would the line move after testing, or immediately in either direction?

**What changes.** The question becomes answerable. She can locate her own reasoning, and either defend the line on competence grounds or recognise that it is doing identity work.

**Is the difference real?** Partly. The test is genuinely clarifying as a self-reflection prompt. But the model itself concedes that this distinction is its hardest judgement and cannot be made reliably from one observation. As a question to sit with it works; as a determination it does not.

**Honest verdict:** moderate value, and only in the reflective mode. Anyone using this as a diagnostic is overreaching.

## S5. Losing the tool the workflow was built around

**Situation.** A consultant has spent a year building a working method around a specific model. It is deprecated. The replacement behaves differently. He is disproportionately angry.

**Without the model.** Read as an overreaction to a tool change, by himself and by everyone around him.

**What the model supplies.** A year of calibration — knowing where it failed, what it could be trusted with, how to compensate — was tacit skill, and it was tool-specific. The loss is of accumulated competence, not of software. The disproportion is only apparent.

**What changes.** Modestly. The response is legitimised, and the person can plan for it: expect a recalibration period, avoid deep commitment to a single provider, treat the ceiling as needing to be rebuilt rather than assumed.

**Is the difference real?** Small but genuine. This is a real experience with no vocabulary, and the model supplies one.

**Which component did the work.** The tool-specific ceiling conjecture — one of the model's least supported claims, which is worth noting.

---

## S6. Sitting with a fear about the future of the work

**Situation.** A translator is not in acute distress but carries a persistent question about whether the profession survives.

**Without the model.** The question cycles without resolution.

**What the model supplies.** A distinction between an identity problem — the work is changing and my sense of myself has not caught up — and a structural problem — there may be no viable position here. These call for entirely different responses: the first for reflection and recalibration, the second for concrete economic planning.

**What changes.** Possibly the response. Someone treating a structural problem as an identity problem does inner work while their position erodes. Someone treating an identity problem as structural leaves a viable career prematurely.

**Is the difference real?** Yes in principle. But the model cannot tell anyone which case they are in — that is an economic judgement about a labour market, which the model explicitly declines to make. It supplies the distinction and nothing else.

**Honest verdict:** the distinction is worth having. The model should be clear that it stops there.

# Assessment for this group

## What genuinely helps

| Contribution | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| **Domain-specificity** | Strongest. Corrects a damaging self-description at almost no cost |
| **Verification burden** | Strong. Relocates a misattributed problem to where it can be fixed |
| **Delegation levels** | Strong. Makes an implicit choice explicit and adjustable |
| **Naming without pathologising** | Genuine. Normalisation is a real mechanism |
| **Identity versus structure** | Useful distinction, with a firm limit on what follows from it |

## What does not help

- **The position graph.** No scenario needed it. Knowing that one position tends to follow another is not actionable for the person in it, and it invites a forecast the model cannot support.
- **Position codes.** An individual gains nothing from a label and loses something: once the label exists, subsequent self-observation is filtered through it.
- **Attributes and drivers.** Interesting as reflection prompts, unusable as self-diagnosis, and the driver people most need to see in themselves is the one they are least able to see.

## The honest summary

For individuals, the model's value is **vocabulary and permission**, not diagnosis.

The recurring pattern across all six scenarios is the same: someone has interpreted a situational response as a personal deficiency, and the model relocates the cause to something structural and addressable. That is genuinely useful, and it requires perhaps a tenth of the model.

**The delivery implication is significant.** Everything above could be conveyed in a short article. A person in situation S2 needs one paragraph about verification burden, not a fourteen-position framework. The gap between what helps this audience and what the model currently is may be the strongest argument in the whole audit for distilling rather than elaborating.
