# Use Cases — HR and Organisations

Scenarios for HR, people functions, organisational development, and anyone designing an adoption programme.

This is the group where the model's value is most defensive. Most of what it usefully supplies is an argument against something the organisation was about to do.

---

## S1. The workforce readiness assessment

**Situation.** An executive asks HR to assess the organisation's AI readiness, person by person, to target training and identify risk.

**Without the model.** The request is fulfilled, because HR functions exist to fulfil requests like this and vendors exist to supply them. The output is a distribution of employees across categories, derived from self-report under conditions where non-adoption is visibly disfavoured. It is unreliable, it is retained, and it influences decisions for years.

**What the model supplies.** A reasoned refusal with four independent grounds, each sufficient on its own: the framework is untested; the constructs are not performance constructs; measuring individual adoption destroys the signal because people report what is rewarded; and an individual psychological record about an employee creates data-protection exposure that a readiness dashboard does not justify.

Then the redirect: the diagnostic questions that can be answered are about the environment. Where is verification burden rising? Which mandates produce compliance rather than judgement? Where has delegation been required above what people accept? Is refusal safe?

**What changes.** Potentially a great deal — an expensive, invasive, unreliable exercise is replaced by a cheaper one that produces actionable answers. Whether the redirect is accepted depends on whether the executive wanted a diagnosis or a dashboard.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and this may be the model's single most valuable organisational contribution. It is entirely preventive.

**A serious tension.** The model supplies both the refusal and, in its assessment machinery, something that looks like the tool being refused. An HR function reading the full model set will find the position descriptions, the observation guide, and the classifier design, and will reasonably conclude that assessment is available. **The refusal is undermined by the model's own contents.** This is a design problem, not a documentation problem.

## S2. Choosing what to measure

**Situation.** A programme is being stood up and needs metrics. The obvious candidates are licence utilisation, per-person usage, and suggestion acceptance rates.

**Without the model.** Those metrics are adopted, because they are available. Usage becomes a visible expectation. Within a quarter it measures compliance and nothing else, and the organisation has permanently lost the ability to tell whether the investment worked.

**What the model supplies.** A specific argument, framed as self-interest rather than ethics: tracking individual usage destroys the organisation's ability to evaluate its own programme. Plus a workable alternative — flow outcomes, rework rate, review-to-production ratio, cost per merged change — none of which requires measuring individuals.

**What changes.** The metric set, at the point where it is cheapest to change.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and the self-interest framing matters. Ethical arguments against employee measurement are routinely overridden. An argument that the measurement destroys the data the executive wants is harder to dismiss.

## S3. A pattern of sick leave in one department

**Situation.** Absence and attrition have risen in a department that adopted agent tooling early. Exit interviews cite workload and vagueness. Output metrics look fine.

**Without the model.** Attributed to change fatigue or to a manager problem. Neither is actionable and both are wrong.

**What the model supplies.** A specific, measurable, testable hypothesis: output volume rose, review capacity did not, and the difference landed on people as an invisible workload. The test costs nothing — review-to-production ratio is derivable from tooling data the organisation already holds.

**What changes.** A vague cultural diagnosis becomes a measurable one with a defined fix. If the ratio has not moved, the hypothesis is wrong and something else is happening, which is also useful.

**Is the difference real?** Yes, and it is the model's best organisational case. It converts an unfalsifiable explanation into a checkable one, and the check requires no access to anyone's psychology.

## S4. Policy on refusal

**Situation.** An employee declines to use AI for a particular class of work. There is no policy. Their manager is unsure whether this is a performance matter.

**Without the model.** Handled ad hoc, usually by pressure. Others observe the outcome and stop declining. The organisation loses its only source of information about where the tool is unsuitable.

**What the model supplies.** A basis for a policy: declining to use AI for a specific piece of work is a legitimate professional judgement requiring no justification, and the organisation depends on people making it. Separately, mandate exposure — everyone should have used the tools on real work — but not delegation level.

**What changes.** A written position that protects both the individual and the organisation's information flow.

**Is the difference real?** Yes. The distinction between mandating exposure and mandating delegation level is a genuinely useful policy formulation and is not obvious before it is stated.

## S5. The vendor with an assessment product

**Situation.** A vendor offers a psychometric assessment of employee AI readiness, with a maturity model and individual scores.

**Without the model.** Evaluated on features and price. Validation status is not asked about, because it usually is not.

**What the model supplies.** The right questions. What is the evidence base? What is the inter-rater reliability? What are the norms, and on what population? What happens to an individual misclassified by it? Would the vendor put the individual-level output in front of a works council?

**What changes.** A better purchasing decision, and possibly no purchase.

**Is the difference real?** Moderate. Any competent HR professional could ask these questions. The model's contribution is prompting them, and providing a worked example of a framework that answers them honestly — which makes the contrast with one that does not more visible.

---

## S6. Programme design from the start

**Situation.** An organisation is designing an adoption programme with a productivity objective.

**Without the model.** Tooling is procured, training is scheduled, usage is tracked, and success is declared on adoption rates. Whether anything improved is never established, because no baseline was taken.

**What the model supplies.** A sequence: instrument flow metrics before any push; remove individual usage tracking; build verification capacity before raising generation volume; voluntary exposure in low-stakes domains first; support skill-building; watch review burden; re-measure against baseline.

**What changes.** The order. In particular, measuring before pushing — which is the step that gets skipped and is the only thing that makes the programme's value demonstrable afterwards.

**Is the difference real?** Partly. Much of this is general programme discipline that a good change practitioner would apply anyway. The genuinely model-specific contributions are three: sequence by stakes, build verification capacity first, and do not mandate delegation level.

**Honest verdict:** valuable as a package, with perhaps a third of it attributable to the model.

# Assessment for this group

## Where the value is

| Contribution | Type | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refusing individual readiness assessment, with reasons | Prevention | Strongest |
| Metric selection, argued from self-interest | Prevention | Strong |
| Verification burden as a measurable cause of a real problem | Diagnosis | Strong |
| Mandate exposure, not delegation level | Policy | Strong, and non-obvious |
| Making refusal safe | Policy | Strong |
| Sequencing by stakes | Design | Moderate |
| Vendor evaluation questions | Prevention | Moderate |

The pattern is unmistakable: **most of the model's HR value is preventive.** It is worth more for what it stops than for what it enables.

That is a legitimate form of value and it is easy to undersell. Preventing an invasive, unreliable, permanently retained workforce assessment is worth more than most positive interventions an HR function could make in this area. But it should be stated accurately, because a framework whose main contribution is a set of prohibitions is a different product from one that promises insight, and it will be received differently.

## Where it is dangerous

**HR is the second most dangerous reader after managers, and for a compounding reason.** HR functions have the authority to make a framework organisation-wide, the systems to store its output, and the institutional habit of turning descriptions of people into records about people. The failure mode is not one manager misusing a label; it is a position code becoming a field in a system.

The specific risks:

- **Positions entering an HR system.** Once a code sits next to a name in a record, it is a fact for as long as the record exists.
- **The framework legitimising a screening instrument.** A published model with academic-sounding constructs is exactly what a vendor needs to build the product the model prohibits.
- **Aggregate reporting sliding to individual reporting.** Team-level reporting is defensible. The request for a breakdown follows within one reporting cycle, and the threshold that prevents it has to be in code rather than in a guideline.
- **Consent that is not consent.** Employee participation in an employer-run assessment is rarely freely given, and treating it as though it were is where most organisational research goes wrong.

## The honest summary

**For HR the model is close to being an ethics and design document with a psychological framework attached, and the framework is the part that creates the risk.**

The valuable output — the prohibitions, the measurement guidance, the policy formulations, the verification burden diagnosis — requires no positions, no graph, and no assessment of anyone. It could be delivered as a short guidance note and would be more likely to be used.

The position machinery adds nothing to any scenario above and supplies exactly the capability the guidance forbids.

**This is the strongest argument in the audit for separating what is published to whom.** An HR-facing document containing the position descriptions and the observation guide arms the reader with the thing the document is telling them not to do. That is not a caveat problem. It is a packaging decision, and it should be made deliberately.
