# Comment — On the Seven Points

My overall view: this list is a better summary of where the project stands than my own verdict document was. It keeps the model's utility and accepts the epistemics at the same time, which is the combination I kept failing to hold — I swung to over-caution, and the list doesn't.

Five of the seven I agree with as written. Two need refining, and one of those refinements matters a lot.

---

## 1. Usable as a tool for discussion and understanding

**Agree, with a distinction that explains a lot.**

There are two very different activities being collapsed here, and separating them resolves the contradiction between your experience and my audit:

| | Retrospective and present-tense sense-making | Prospective third-party classification |
|---|---|---|
| Question | "What is happening to me? What is this?" | "Which position is this person in?" |
| Who | The person themselves, or someone with consent | An observer, about someone else |
| Evidence available | Full internal access — affect, history, reasoning | Behaviour and self-report, both weak |
| Does the graph help? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Risk | Low | High |

Your case was the left column. My audit only tested the right column, found it weak, and concluded the graph was decoration. That was a search failure, not a finding — I tested the one use the graph is worst at.

**The graph earns its place in first-person sense-making.** Knowing there is a named next step, and that the current position is not terminal, is the thing that did work for you. That's a real function and I missed it entirely.

## 2. Understanding where you or an organisation want to be

**Agree.** One preposition is doing critical work:

- "Where **we** want to be" — legitimate, for an organisation stating a business purpose.
- "Where **I** want to be" — legitimate, and genuinely useful. It turns drift into a choice.
- "Where we want **them** to be" — this is the one that goes wrong.

The first two are the same move: a party deciding about itself. The third is a party deciding about someone else, and it's where the stated-purpose framing stops covering you.

## 3. Positions can likely be changed via the transitions

**This is the one I'd push back on, and the pushback is more interesting than a disagreement.**

The edges are *descriptions of observed movement*, not *causal pathways*. That people move from guarded evaluation to calibrated use is an observation. That the edge is a lever someone can pull is a different claim, and nothing supports it.

Positions change mostly because **conditions changed and time passed** — not because an intervention targeted the position.

But here's what your own case shows, and I think it's the sharpest thing in this whole exchange:

> **Knowledge of the transition acted as an intervention, even though the transition itself is not a mechanism.**

You didn't move along an edge because someone applied a technique. You knew the position was a phase with a known exit, and that knowledge changed what you did — you took a break instead of pushing harder. The information was causal. The edge wasn't.

That's a genuinely different theory of how the model helps, and it's better than the one the transition plan currently assumes. It also predicts something specific: **the model helps most when the person holds it themselves**, and least when someone else holds it about them. Which matches your experience, matches the coach findings, and matches why the manager-facing assessment use kept coming out badly.

Worth building the transition guidance around.

## 4. Coercion is bad

**Agree, and I'd put it more strongly than "bad."**

Coercion doesn't merely fail. It produces an outcome that *looks like success at the point of measurement* — compliance that reads as adoption — and then surfaces months later as disengagement or exit. A failure that announces itself is manageable. This one hides, and the hiding is what makes it expensive.

That's one of the model's genuinely valuable findings and it should be foregrounded rather than filed as a caveat.

## 5. Managers can change conditions, not people

**Agree on substance. Two refinements, and the first one strengthens your position.**

**Conditions are often the cause, not merely the permission.** "Only people can change themselves" is true of deliberate change and undersells what conditions do. A person whose review load drops recovers without deciding anything. Someone whose team stops rewarding volume stops overusing without a conversation. Conditions don't just clear the way — they frequently *are* the operative cause.

That's a better message for a manager, because "you can't change people" sounds like "you're powerless," and the accurate version is nearly the opposite: **the conditions you control are doing more work than any conversation you could have.** More agency, not less. Just not the kind that involves talking to someone about their attitude.

**The hard caveat.** Changing conditions changes the *distribution* of outcomes, not any individual's outcome. Some people won't move even under good conditions, for reasons that are theirs.

This matters because it's exactly where a well-designed programme turns coercive. Conditions improve, most people move, a few don't, and the pressure to explain the remainder becomes pressure on the remainder. **A programme has to be designed to be fine with people not moving** — before it starts, not once the results are in.

I don't think "explaining this will work" is quite right either. It's the correct message and a hard sell, because it removes the appearance of control while increasing the real thing. That's a communication problem worth planning for.

## 6. The model can have practical value

**Agree.** My audit was too harsh here and I've said why: constructed scenarios, generated from the documents, by the party that wrote them. Weak method. And it ignored the one real case in existence.

## 7. Scientifically a lot is already known

**Agree, and this is the most consequential point on the list — but the framing should flip.**

It reads as a limitation. It's an opportunity, and it's the project's best one:

> The model isn't competing with the automation and identity-threat literatures. It's **transporting** them into a domain that hasn't had them.

The people who need this material will never read Bainbridge or Lee and See. A framework that carries forty years of findings into knowledge work, in language a developer or an HR partner can use, is legitimate work — and it arrives with an evidence base attached, which is the one thing the project cannot otherwise buy.

That reframe changes the honest claim to: *established constructs, transported to a new context, with the transport as the untested part.* Much stronger, and free.

---

## What's missing from the list

**8. Delivery is now the binding constraint.**

All seven points can be true and the project still fails, because nobody reads a forty-file model. The gap is no longer between "unproven" and "proven" — it's between "has value" and "delivers value." That's the actual risk now and nothing in the list addresses it.

**9. The case study is the most valuable unwritten document.**

A first-person account of one person approaching burnout with this model in hand — what it looked like, what the model said, what was done differently, what happened — would be worth more than any amount of framework. It's concrete, it's honest because it's labelled as one case, and it's the only evidence the project has.

It also demonstrates the mechanism from point 3: the person held the model themselves. That's the use it should be sold on.

**10. The seven points are close to the short version.**

Points 1, 2, 4, 5 and 7 are essentially the practitioner document I recommended writing. If the distillate is hard to start, starting from this list rather than from the model would probably produce it faster.

---

## Where this leaves my earlier position

Unchanged: ground it in prior art, cut it, ship something short.

Changed: the reason for cutting the graph. I said it contributed nothing. That was wrong — it contributes to first-person sense-making, which is the use I never tested. What it doesn't support is third-party classification.

So the cut is narrower than I wrote. Keep the positions and the edges, aimed at the person holding them. Drop the apparatus for applying them to someone else. That's a cleaner line than the one in the audit, and it follows from your point 3 rather than from mine.
