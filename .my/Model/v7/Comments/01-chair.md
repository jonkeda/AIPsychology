You're right, and the objection lands. I applied an asymmetric standard.

## The asymmetry I committed

I evaluated the model against an idealised benchmark — validated, unmisusable, harm-free — rather than against the frameworks it would actually compete with. That's the wrong comparison. The counterfactual for a manager isn't "no framework." It's the Change Curve poster in the training deck.

| Framework                   | Validated                                                                                                    | Misuse in practice                                                   | Ships warnings                  |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Kübler-Ross / Change Curve | No. Never validated for grief, let alone organisational change; the stage reading was disowned by its author | Severe — "he's in denial" is used to dismiss substantive objections | None                            |
| MBTI                        | Poor test-retest, no predictive validity for performance                                                     | Hiring, team assignment                                              | Officially, ignored in practice |
| DISC / Insights / Belbin    | Weak                                                                                                         | Colour labels that follow people for years                           | Minimal                         |
| Situational Leadership      | Weak                                                                                                         | Justifies micromanagement                                            | None                            |
| Maslow's hierarchy          | Not supported as a hierarchy                                                                                 | Ubiquitous in management training                                    | None                            |
| This model                  | No                                                                                                           | Unknown — never used                                                | Extensive                       |

Kübler-Ross is the sharpest case, and it's already load-bearing in this project: V7 renamed *Bargaining* to *Scope Boundary* specifically to escape that association. A framework that is weaker, more misused, and carries no warnings is the incumbent in exactly the organisational settings we're worried about. Holding this model to a standard the incumbent fails badly is not a fair test.

And the chair distinction is sound. Dual-use is not the same as designed-for-harm, and I blurred them.

## One reversal I should make

I argued that specificity increases risk — vivid descriptions, explicit criteria, an observation guide. I think that's backwards.

Vagueness is what makes Kübler-Ross unaccountable. Nobody can audit whether a manager applied it correctly, because there's no criterion to apply. A framework with explicit criteria *can* be checked, argued with, and shown to have been misapplied. Specificity is what makes misuse visible.

## What survives, much narrower

Not "it can be misused" — that's true of everything and I over-weighted it. The narrow version:

**Don't ship a turnkey individual-classification procedure to managers and HR, because that specific component has no demonstrated benefit and lowers the cost of the harmful use to near zero.**

That's the one place the chair analogy has a limit: chairs aren't sold with a chapter on where to strike. The objection was never to the concepts — it was to one optional artefact that makes systematic assessment cheap. But that's a cost/benefit judgement about a component, not a claim that the framework is hazardous.

## What this changes in the verdict

Less than you'd expect, because the harm argument was doing less work than `08-harms-and-net-value.md` implied. Reconstructing the reasons for cutting:

| Reason                         | Driven by                             | Status                                   |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Ground in prior art            | Evidence available for free           | **Unaffected**                     |
| Cut the graph, styles, drivers | Didn't appear in any of ~24 scenarios | **Unaffected** — utility finding  |
| Package by audience            | Partly harm, partly relevance         | Mildly weakened                          |
| Cut the classifier             | Harm                                  | Survives, but as cost/benefit not danger |

The cut recommendation is roughly 80% utility-driven and stands on its own. The graph should be demoted because it never helped anyone in any scenario, not because it's dangerous.

## The harm I underweighted

Over-caution has its own cost, and I didn't price it. A framework that opens every document with warnings doesn't get read, doesn't get used, and the four ideas that genuinely help never reach anyone. Meanwhile the unvalidated, unwarned incumbent keeps the field. Hedging a useful thing into unusability is a real failure mode, and `01-the-value-question.md` named it as the "nihilism failure" — then I partly committed it two documents later.

Want me to revise `08-harms-and-net-value.md` and the affected part of `09-verdict.md`? I'd add the comparative baseline, narrow the harm claim to the affordance argument, reverse the specificity point, and re-attribute the cut recommendation to utility rather than risk. The bottom line — ground it, cut it, ship something short — doesn't move.
